Commercial printing is not one trade. The shop that can hold color across a 20,000-piece offset run is rarely the shop you want carving a letterpress poster, and neither of them is set up to put vinyl on a truck. This list covers ten Middle Tennessee printers that are each clearly the right answer to a specific question — and says which question.

The ten are listed alphabetically and nothing here is numbered. Ordering a narrow-web label converter against a museum letterpress shop would produce a number that means nothing, so the page declines to produce one.

Nothing here is ranked, so there is no position to sell. The ten shops were researched and added on merit, none of them paid to appear, none was contacted before publication, and no link on this page is an affiliate link. How to choose a print shop

First work out whether you want a printer or a converter. These are different trades that share a word. Crown Label runs narrow-web presses and makes roll labels; LedBetter cuts vinyl and wraps trucks; Franklin Web runs non-heatset web for publications; Hatch Show Print carves blocks. None of them is a worse version of the others, and asking a label converter for business cards wastes everybody's afternoon. Four of the ten shops on this page will decline your job outright, and that is the system working.

Ask where the bindery is. Folding, stitching, die-cutting and binding are where schedules break, because a subcontracted bindery means the job leaves the building, waits in somebody else's queue, and comes back with nobody accountable for the damage. That's Printing and Pollock both keep finishing in-house — worth knowing before you compare their quote against a shop that doesn't and looks cheaper on paper.

If color is contractual, ask about G7 and ask for a press check. A monitor proof is a suggestion; screens are not calibrated to each other, let alone to a press. Phillips holds G7 Master certification, which is the specific answer to "will the reprint match" — if your job is a brand book or an annual report, that certification is the thing to ask other shops whether they hold too.

For mail, the print is the easy half. Postal rules decide whether a drop goes out on time, and they are not the printer's problem unless the printer makes them their problem. Joyner & Hogan have been doing forms and direct mail since 1937, Franklin Web ink-jets and inserts in-house, Pollock warehouses and ships. Ask who is presorting, who is holding the permit, and what happens if the list is dirty.

What to send your printer

  • PDF/X, not a Word file, not a JPEG, not a Canva link.
  • CMYK, not RGB — RGB will be converted for you, and you will not like how.
  • 300 dpi at final printed size. Not 300 dpi at thumbnail size scaled up.
  • 0.125" bleed on every side that runs to the edge.
  • Critical text and logos 0.125–0.25" clear of the trim.
  • Fonts embedded or text outlined.
  • Spot colors called out by PMS number if a brand color has to be exact.
  • Finished size and quantity written down, in the file name if nowhere else.

Questions worth asking before a first order

  • Is the bindery in this building, or does the job leave?
  • Can I see a hard proof, and can I stand at the press?
  • What is the real turnaround, and what does rush actually cost?
  • Who handles the mail drop and the postal permit?
  • What file format do you actually want from me?

Frequently asked questions

Digital or offset — which do I want?

Short runs and anything where each piece differs go digital. Long runs go offset: the plates cost money up front and then the per-piece price falls off a cliff, and offset holds color more tightly. The crossover depends on the piece, so ask for both numbers rather than guessing.

What is a bleed, and why does my printer keep asking about it?

It is artwork extending past the trim line, usually by 0.125 inches, so that when the guillotine wanders by a hair you get color to the edge instead of a white sliver. Trimming is never perfect. Bleed is how you make imperfect trimming invisible.

Is there a minimum order?

Digital work often has none. Offset effectively does, because the plate and makeready costs have to be spread across something — below a few thousand pieces the per-unit price stops making sense against digital.

Can I supply my own paper?

Sometimes, and it is usually a false economy. The shop knows how its own stock behaves through its own presses, and once you supply the paper, spoilage is your problem rather than theirs.

How is a print job actually quoted?

Quantity, flat and finished size, number of colors, stock, and finishing — with finishing the line that most often doubles a job people thought was simple. This is why no page, including this one, can honestly publish a starting price for commercial print.
