Overton County, Tennessee

Things to do in Livingston

A county seat of under four thousand people, fifteen miles north of Cookeville, with a state park, a famously clear lake and a courthouse square you can walk in an afternoon. Here is what is actually here.

Where to start

Start on the square

Livingston is a county seat, and county seats keep their business in one place. The Overton County Courthouse went up in 1868-69, replacing the one that burned during the Civil War, and it is on the National Register of Historic Places. Everything downtown is arranged around it, close enough that you can park once and walk the rest.

Two blocks off the square, the Overton County Heritage Museum occupies the county's old sheriff's office and jail. It opened in March 2002 as a project of the Overton County Historical Society, and the collection includes one of roughly thirty-five surviving iron lungs in the country. Hours are short, so check before you drive up.

On foot

The Livingston Mural Trail is a self-guided walk that starts from the Greetings from Overton County mural on the courthouse square. The Antique Market, also on the square, holds booths from more than forty dealers, and the Millard Oakley Public Library is a block from it, free, and a reasonable place to sit out a wet afternoon.

Two parks in town

Central Park Livingston opened downtown in 2017 with a guitar-shaped splash pad, a walking loop and an amphitheater that runs the Live in Livingston concert series in warm months. Livingston City Park (Recreation Park / City Ballpark) is the working end of the same idea: ball fields, pickleball courts, walking paths and a public pool in summer.

Also worth knowing

Overton County Arts runs classes, bimonthly Art Mixers and a K-12 program, and much of it is open to the public. The Livingston-Overton County Farmers Market runs seasonally from May through November. Out on Old Highway 42 between Livingston and Rickman, Roaring River Church and Cemetery is one of the oldest burial grounds in the county, founded by Revolutionary War veteran Colonel Stephen Copeland in the late 1700s with the congregation chartered in 1801. There are no facilities there. It is a stop, not a site.

Out of town

Standing Stone and the woods

About ten miles northwest of the square, Standing Stone State Park covers 855 acres around a 69-acre lake made by damming Mill Creek. The stonework is CCC-era and it is what most people remember about the place. There are trails, rental cabins, a campground and rowboats in season, and day use is free. Give it an afternoon.

Wrapped around the park, Standing Stone State Forest (WMA) adds nearly 11,000 acres managed by TWRA. It is open for hunting and dog training in season and for walking and wildlife watching the rest of the year. Check TWRA season dates before you go, and wear orange during big-game seasons.

Rolley Hole

Standing Stone hosts the National Rolley Hole Marbles Championship each September. It is a serious tournament of a game most of the country has never heard of, and it is the single most Overton County thing on this page.

If you brought a machine

Cub Mountain ATV Park & Campground at Crawford runs about fifty miles of trails across roughly 1,900 acres, with a stocked four-acre fishing lake, waterfalls, old coal-mining sites and camping. Admission is paid. If you would rather stay in the car, the Cumberland National Scenic Byway - Overton County segment runs SR 42 south of town and SR 52 northwest toward the park, passing the square on the way. For nine holes, Hidden Valley Golf & Country Club has been in Livingston since 1966 and is open to the public most days, members and guests only on Sundays.

The big one

Out on the water

Dale Hollow is a 27,700-acre reservoir on the Obey River, and the water is clear enough to see the bottom in the shallows. It has a national reputation for smallmouth bass. Overton County's own access point is Dale Hollow Lake - Overton County access (Mitchell Creek Marina / Livingston Boat Dock) near Allons, which older maps and older people still call the Livingston Boat Dock.

For staying the night on the lake, Obey River Park Campground near Monroe is a Corps of Engineers park with more than 120 sites, the largest swimming beach on Dale Hollow, a boat ramp, and bald eagles in winter. Reservations go through recreation.gov and the season runs roughly mid-April to late October.

Past the city limits

Out in the county

Overton County is bigger than its county seat, and most of it is communities of a few hundred people with a post office, a church and a road running through. Alpine, Hilham, Rickman, Monroe, Allons, Crawford, Timothy, Hardy's Chapel. None of them are attractions. All of them are worth the drive if you want to see what the county actually looks like.

Alpine

The community took the name Alpine in 1921, after the Alpine Institute, a mission school that ran at various points through the 19th and early 20th centuries. The campus is still legible: a native-limestone gymnasium built by the WPA in 1939, the old woodshop, the manse, and the 1934 Christ Church Presbyterian building, with farmland running up to the edge of it.

Hilham

Hilham sits on State Route 85 west of Livingston, on the way to Standing Stone. There is a Tennessee Historical Commission marker there for the Fisk Female Academy, a post office, a Methodist church, and Claude Lee's Service Station still standing on the highway.

Crawford

Crawford was a coal community. What is left includes an abandoned general store and former post office, photographed by Jack Corn for the federal government in April 1974 and now in the National Archives. Cub Mountain's ATV trails run on the old mining ground above it.

One more marker

A Tennessee Historical Commission marker in Livingston honors Lester Flatt, the bluegrass musician, who was born at Duncan's Chapel in Overton County on 19 June 1914.

Eating

Where to eat

There is no restaurant district here. There are locally owned kitchens, most of them within a few blocks of the courthouse, and most of them done serving before a city dinner hour. Eat early.

Plates and barbecue

Stover's Country Kitchen serves breakfast all day alongside hamburger steak, pinto beans and cornbread, and it is the closest thing to a default answer in town. For barbecue there are two different arguments: 1806 BBQ sits right on the courthouse square doing pulled pork, brisket, wings and a salad bar, while Brazen Que cooks Texas-style over live oak. Casa Mexicana covers Tex-Mex near the square, and Angelina's Italian Subs & More is a counter making Italian subs and pasta from family recipes.

Coffee and afternoon

Wired Coffee Co has been going since 2017 on espresso, cold brew and a chicken salad sandwich people order by name. Ruby & Lee Coffee and Bakehouse is on the square inside the Market on Main boutique, doing cinnamon rolls, muffins and breakfast sandwiches. In summer, The Sno Shack is a walk-up shaved ice stand with a short food menu attached, afternoons and evenings, closed Sundays.

Timing

The town's calendar

Overton County runs on annual events more than on year-round attractions, so when you come changes what there is to do. The Overton County Fair is the county's agricultural fair at the fairgrounds, with cattle and livestock shows, pageants and wild mule shows.

In autumn, Fall-O-Ween in Livingston spreads across the historic square and Central Park from September into late October, finishing with a Halloween weekend of trick-or-treating. Paint Livingston, run by Overton County Arts, brings plein air painters from several states to work around town and ends with a Quick Paint contest on the square. In December the Livingston-Overton County Christmas Parade comes down Main Street with floats, marching bands and Santa.

Dates move

Every event on this page shifts year to year. Check the organizer before you plan a trip around one.

If you only have one day

One good day in Livingston

Livingston is small enough to see properly in a day, if you take it in the right order. Start on the square, get out of town by early afternoon, and be back for supper.

  • Morning

    Overton County Courthouse and the square

    Start where the town starts. The 1868-69 brick courthouse is on the National Register, and the block around it holds most of what is worth walking to — the antique dealers, the coffee counters, the Greetings from Overton County mural. Half an hour on foot, longer if you shop.

  • Late morning

    Overton County Heritage Museum

    Two blocks off the square, in the county's old sheriff's office and jail. It is a small local museum run by the historical society, and it holds an iron lung — one of roughly thirty-five left in the country. Check the hours before you drive up; they are short.

  • Noon

    Stover's Country Kitchen

    Lunch, plainly. Breakfast is served all day, and the rest is hamburger steak, pinto beans and cornbread. Small-town kitchens here close early, so eat before one.

  • Afternoon

    Standing Stone State Park

    Ten miles northwest, and the reason most people leave the highway. An 855-acre park around a 69-acre lake, with CCC stonework, trails off the lake loop, and rowboats in season. Free to enter. Give it two hours minimum, or the whole afternoon.

  • Evening

    Central Park Livingston

    Back in town. A 2017 downtown park with a splash pad, a walking loop and an amphitheater that runs the Live in Livingston series in warm months. Free, and it is where the evening actually happens.

Before you go

Know before you go

Where is Livingston, and how do I get there?

Livingston is the county seat of Overton County, in the Upper Cumberland region of Middle Tennessee, approximately 15 miles north-northeast of Cookeville. Cookeville is the nearest town on Interstate 40, so most visitors exit there and drive north. There is no commercial air service or passenger rail in the county.

What is the best season to visit?

Spring and fall are the most comfortable for anything outdoors. Summer is hot and humid, and the water — lake and creek — is the point of the season. Winter is quiet and cold; some seasonal attractions and campgrounds reduce hours or close, so call ahead between November and March.

Do I need a car?

Yes. Points of interest are spread across a rural county, there is no meaningful public transit, and the distances between the square, the state park and the lake are driving distances, not walking ones.

What should I know about small-town hours?

Many businesses here keep weekday hours, close early, and are closed Sundays; some close Saturday afternoons as well. Kitchens in particular often stop serving well before big-city dinner hours. Check hours the day you go rather than trusting a listing — including the ones on this page.

Where do people stay?

Lodging in the county runs to a small number of motels, cabins and short-term rentals, plus campgrounds and lakeside sites in season. Visitors wanting a wider choice of chain hotels generally stay in Cookeville and drive up.

Is the town walkable?

The courthouse square is, and that is the part worth walking — the museum, library, shops and several kitchens are within a few blocks of each other. Everything past the square assumes a car.
